# The Weird Outlier Rule

**Every ideation shortlist gets at least one weird outlier. The criteria do not get to veto it.**

## What a weird outlier is

A weird outlier is an idea the team would never have generated alone. It usually:

- Came from a quieter voice in the room
- Was proposed half-jokingly and the room laughed
- Looks low-feasibility, low-impact, or low-novelty on the criteria
- But has a real intuition behind it that's worth testing

Examples from the book's worked examples and adapted:

- Gamified team compliance leaderboards by department (CS voice, half-joking, room laughed)
- A "do nothing for 90 days" option in a roadmap where every other item is "build something"
- A workshop redesign that requires a senior leader to participate fully (politically improbable but might be the right answer)

## Why every shortlist needs one

Three reasons:

1. **Signal-to-noise of the room.** The room generated this idea. Killing it because it scored low on conservative criteria sends a message that the team's creativity won't survive contact with the synthesis. The team will generate less freely next time.

2. **Low-cost option value.** A weird outlier kept as "research first" or "monitor" costs almost nothing in a quarter. If it turns out to be the seed of a real insight, you'll be glad you kept it.

3. **Reads the session generously.** A shortlist without a weird outlier reads as the synthesizer dismissing what was unusual about the team's thinking. Even if you kill the outlier at the next gate, the inclusion at this gate honors the room.

## How to keep it without breaking the shortlist

The outlier doesn't go into the top three. The top three are non-negotiable on criteria. The outlier goes into the shortlist with one of these framings:

- **Research first** — small N, low-cost test. *If the pilot works, fascinating; if not, we learn something cheap.*
- **Monitor** — kept on the radar; revisit in 90 days when conditions might change.
- **One-team pilot** — limit scope to one team or one segment to bound the cost.

## When to kill the outlier

The next gate. Not this one.

If the outlier still scores low after a research-first cycle, retire it then. The point of keeping it on the shortlist is that the team got one cycle to test it. If the test failed, the team learned something. **The cost was low and the credibility gain was real.**

## When NOT to apply this rule

When the outlier doesn't have a real intuition behind it. *I think we should rebrand the company* in an ideation session about onboarding flow is not a weird outlier — it's off-topic. Off-topic ideas go to the kill list under "Out of scope."

The rule is for ideas the team would never have generated alone but that *do* sit on the topic. Not for tangents.

## When the synthesizer wants to kill the outlier

If you find yourself wanting to cut the weird outlier because it doesn't fit the criteria, ask:

- Was the criteria the team agreed to *too conservative* for this session? Sometimes the room's intent was generative, but the criteria were operational. The criteria can be wrong.
- Is the outlier on-topic? If yes, keep it. If no, kill it.
- Would you regret cutting it in a quarter? If yes, keep it.

## What the rule prevents

A shortlist where everything is feasible, near-term, low-novelty, and on-criteria. That shortlist reads as the synthesizer's risk-aversion, not the room's potential.
