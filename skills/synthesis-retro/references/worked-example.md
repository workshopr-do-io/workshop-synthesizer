# Retro Recipe — Worked Example

*Composite — assembled from a longer string of team retros I've run between 2015 and 2025. The trust-sticky moment is a pattern I've seen often enough to lift into the open; the specific team is not one team.*

The full worked example — *the trust sticky* — is in the book, Chapter 8.

## Setup

Eleven-person engineering team. Q3 retro. The "what should change" column had two clear winners by dot count — *cross-team communication* (8 dots) and *prioritization clarity* (6 dots) — and one sticky with **1 dot** that read, in lower case:

> *we don't trust each other on decisions.*

The handwriting belonged to a senior engineer who'd been quiet through most of the discussion. I'd watched her write it during silent generation, look at it for a beat, post it on the wall, and not advocate for it.

I knew, walking out, that the trust sticky was the most important sticky on the wall.

## Step 1 — Cluster

Three columns. The clusterer ran clean. The minority-view pass flagged the trust sticky inside the cross-team handoffs cluster with a note: *this sticky may not belong to the cross-team handoffs theme; it may be its own theme.*

Good catch.

## Step 2 — Dissent vs. noise

Model classified the trust sticky as "real structural dissent." Its framing: *one team member is naming a pattern of decision-making that doesn't get ratified across the team, which is being treated as a cross-team handoff problem but may actually be an internal-team-trust problem.*

The right reading.

I went back to the discussion recording. The senior engineer had spoken once, halfway through. She'd said: *"the handoff problems are real, but I think we should be careful that we're not just blaming the other teams. Sometimes we don't agree internally before we go to them, and that's a different problem."* The room had nodded politely and moved on.

The discussion didn't address the trust theme either. The dot vote didn't surface it. The cluster initially folded it into cross-team. **Three different forces, all flattening the same observation.**

## Step 3 — Interpretation

> **Cross-team handoffs and internal alignment.** *What didn't / what should change.* The dominant view is that cross-team dependencies, especially with the Platform team, caused friction throughout the quarter, and the team wants clearer handoff protocols. There is a related minority view, raised by one team member but not addressed in the discussion: that the team sometimes goes to cross-team conversations without internal alignment first, which makes the handoffs look like external problems when they may originate as internal disagreements about decisions. The proposed changes from the "what should change" column focus on handoff protocols. The minority view suggests a parallel investment: a regular internal decision-ratification practice. Both may be needed.

## Step 4 — Action items (5)

1. EM owns a cross-team handoff working session within 30 days
2. PM owns rewriting the team's quarterly prioritization doc to address the "clarity" gap
3. Tech lead owns a tech-debt allocation policy (20% of sprint capacity, starting next sprint)
4. **EM owns a regular internal alignment practice — a recurring 30-min weekly session, starting this month.** *This addresses the minority-view concern from the cross-team theme.*
5. Senior on-call engineer owns a written escalation policy, two weeks

One of the action items — the internal alignment practice — explicitly addresses the trust dissent without naming it as a trust problem. **That's the move.** The dissent gets a structural response without putting any team member on the spot.

## Step 5 — Public write-up

The minority-view paragraph in the cross-team section:

> *There's a related observation from the room that didn't get airtime in the discussion but appeared in the stickies: that some of the cross-team friction may originate as internal disagreement that hasn't been resolved before the handoff. The proposed action items below include a regular internal alignment practice that addresses this directly.*

## Pre-send check

Sent the draft to the senior engineer (who wrote the trust sticky) and the EM. She replied with one line:

> *"Thank you for hearing it."*

EM replied: *"Yes, this reads fair."*

Published Tuesday morning.

## Result

Team standup Wednesday focused most heavily on the prioritization action item — the headline by dot count, as expected. The EM ran the first internal alignment session two weeks later. Three sprints into the next quarter, the team's mid-quarter feelings score came in at 4.1 — a real lift.

That's a retro synthesis that protected the dissent without making it the headline.

## What this example teaches

- **A 1-dot sticky can be the most important sticky on the wall.** Dot counts measure noise. They don't measure signal.
- **Three different forces flatten the same observation.** Discussion didn't address it. Dot vote didn't surface it. Cluster initially folded it. The synthesis is what catches the flattening.
- **Reframe rather than confront.** The action item addressed the dissent structurally without naming it as a trust problem. The team got the right intervention without anyone being put on the spot.
- **The pre-send check is non-optional.** Two messages from the room — *"thank you for hearing it"* and *"yes, this reads fair"* — meant the write-up could publish.
