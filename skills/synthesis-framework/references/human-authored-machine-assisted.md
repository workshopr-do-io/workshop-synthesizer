# Human-Authored, Machine-Assisted

The principle that holds the whole stack together. If you forget the matrix, forget the moves, forget the stack — keep this one.

> **Human-authored, machine-assisted.**

The model assists. The human authors. The deliverable has your name on it, not the model's. The interpretation comes out of your head. The ranking comes out of your judgment. The story comes out of your relationship with the room.

## Why this matters

Synthesis is the place where authorship and consequence touch.

- *You* are accountable for what the deck says.
- *You* are the one who walks into the readout meeting on Monday and gets the question.

If the model wrote the call, you can't defend the call. If you defer to the model on the part that matters, you stop being a facilitator and start being a translator.

## The two failure modes the principle prevents

### The slop deliverable

The model ghostwrites, the human rubber-stamps, the client smells it. Usually happens when the human is tired, the deadline is close, and the model's output is "good enough" by an internal-relief standard rather than a client-recognition standard.

A real example from the book: a senior consultant — twenty years in the business, three big-name books, a roster of Fortune 100 clients — got walked out of a project because the synthesis was AI-flavored and the client could feel it. The client couldn't articulate what was wrong. They just said, "I don't see your hand in this." The relationship ended that week.

### The manual martyr

The human refuses model help and burns the weekend. The synthesis is honest and the human is wrecked. This failure feels virtuous. It is not. It is unsustainable.

The principle gets you between these two. The model does the parts where the model is good. The human does the parts where the human is irreplaceable.

## What "authorship" actually means in practice

Six concrete tests:

- The interpretation of each cluster is in **your sentences**, not the model's.
- The order of the readout is **your choice**, not the model's.
- The call at the top is **yours**, written in your voice, defensible by you.
- The line that frames the work is **yours**.
- The evidence — the cluster, the quotes — can come from anywhere, including the model.
- The prose can be model-drafted and human-tightened.

When the client asks you why, you can answer every question from "why is this first" to "why this word, not that one" without ever saying "the model suggested it." If you can do that, you're authoring. If you can't, go back and re-author until you can.

## Disclosing AI use to clients

Three patterns:

### Always tell them in principle, at the start

The frame goes in the kickoff email, the engagement letter, the pitch. One line: *My synthesis workflow uses AI for clustering and drafting; the interpretation and authorship are mine.*

Most clients today either assume this already or will assume it the next time they read about your craft. Setting the frame in advance removes a small problem.

### Don't narrate it during the work

Don't say *I asked Claude what it thought* in the readout meeting. That sentence reads as deference. The synthesis is yours. The model is one of your tools.

### Disclose specifically when the model's contribution changed the call

*I ran the transcripts through a clustering pass and a contrarian theme surfaced that I want to flag* is honest about a specific contribution and frames you as using the tool well, not offloading judgment. That kind of disclosure builds credibility.

The pattern under all three: **don't hide, don't lead with it. Use it, own the result, be specific about its contribution when it mattered.**

## What about your own work?

If you're writing a book or producing public-facing content about synthesis, the same standards apply to you. Disclose what the model did and didn't do in your own work. Be specific. "Sparring partner" is too vague; the book's own standards demand more.
