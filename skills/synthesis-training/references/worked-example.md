# Training Debrief Recipe — Worked Example

*Composite — drawn from a handful of cohort-based leadership programs I've run between 2019 and 2025. Cohort counts, pulse scores, and the specific participant outcomes below are illustrative of typical patterns, not measured from a single program. The "twelve of sixteen" sort of number you'll see in this example is the kind of number a real cohort report contains, written here at a level of specificity meant to be teachable. Replace with your own.*

The full worked example — *the first-time-manager cohort* — is in the book, Chapter 9.

## Setup

Sixteen first-time managers from a 600-person product company. The L&D leader had been running this program twice a year and wanted a synthesis sharp enough to make program changes for next cohort and to defend the program to the VP of People at the upcoming budget review.

Three core competencies covered: difficult conversations, managing ambiguity, developing direct reports.

Saturday morning. Synthesis-ready file held the artifacts, pulse data, closing-circle recording, program design.

## Step 1 — Baseline-to-close pairs

Four hours. Sixteen participants, each with a paragraph capturing what they wanted and what they took.

The pairs surfaced three patterns I hadn't fully seen during the program:

1. **Four participants** had baseline expectations focused on tactics (*how do I run a 1:1, how do I give feedback*) and left with frame-level shifts (*I now think about my job as making my team's work possible, not as doing my own work plus theirs*).
2. **Three participants** had baseline expectations around career advancement (*I want to be a great manager so I can be promoted*) and left with management-as-craft framings (*I want to be a great manager because I think it's the work I most want to be doing*).
3. **Two participants** had baseline expectations around their teams (*I want to handle X situation with Y direct report*) and left with management-fit clarity — one realizing they wanted to keep managing, one realizing they didn't.

## Step 2 — Cohort-pattern surfacer

Model found two sub-cohorts I hadn't named:

- **Experience-heavy** (six people with 6+ years IC tenure) moved further on difficult-conversations content
- **Experience-light** (ten people with 3 or fewer) moved further on managing-ambiguity content

Made sense in retrospect — experience-heavy managers had accumulated avoided conversations; experience-light managers were swimming in ambiguity for the first time.

Model also surfaced two below-average participants: one told by his skip-level the program was mandatory (attended without buying in); one processing a family situation that ate his attention all three days. I verified both reads.

## Step 3 — Program-design signals

The session on **managing ambiguity** — which the program owner and I had agreed was the strongest-designed — landed with mixed pulse scores.

The session on **developing direct reports** — which we'd designed in a rush and were both unsure about — landed with the strongest scores.

Hypothesis after sitting with it: *developing direct reports* was the most exercise-heavy. Participants did three peer-coaching rounds. *Managing ambiguity* was more frameworks-and-discussion. Participants engaged intellectually but didn't demonstrate the skill as often.

**The demonstration-vs-discussion distinction predicted the pulse outcome.**

That insight became the headline recommendation: *more demonstration, less discussion, in every session that's currently underweight on exercise.*

## Step 4 — Participant write-ups (16 of them)

The personalized paragraph for the tactics-to-frame-shift participant:

> *On day two, when you stopped the role-play to ask what your direct report would need from you to do their best work, and then sat with the silence for a beat before answering — that was the moment I want to point to. You were doing the work the role-play was meant to teach, in real time, on yourself. The frame you're working on this quarter is going to test that capacity to pause. What I'd most recommend you practice between now and your 30-day check-in is to keep doing what you did in that pause.*

Specific. Cited a real moment. Closed with a concrete practice. The participant replied two weeks later saying he'd reread that paragraph three times.

## Step 5 — Cohort analysis

Nine pages. Flags section named both below-average participants with specific framings.

For the participant who'd been told to attend, I wrote: *The signal here isn't about the program — it's about how this person came into the program. The recommendation is to add a one-line pre-program screening question to the application: "What do you most want from this experience?" Participants who can't answer specifically may be poor fits.*

That recommendation became one of three program-design changes for the next cohort.

## Step 6 — Budget-holder one-pager

Two named stories:

1. The participant who came in for tactics and left with the frame shift
2. The participant who came in expecting management training and left with the realization that he didn't want to manage

I framed the second carefully:

> *The program enabled a high-value re-routing decision: the company is retaining a strong individual contributor instead of promoting a poor manager. Cost of the program: 1/20th of one bad-manager mis-hire.*

That framing made the one-pager survive its retelling at the VP's leadership meeting.

## Result

L&D leader implemented two of three program-design changes for the next cohort. Pulse scores on the redesigned managing-ambiguity sessions came back stronger. VP of People referenced the named stories at her quarterly business review.

That's a training debrief that paid for itself.

## What this example teaches

- **The baseline-to-close pair is the spine.** Without it, the synthesis reports on outputs (artifacts produced) instead of outcomes (people changed).
- **The strongest-designed session sometimes lands less than the rushed one.** Demonstration vs. discussion is a better predictor than design intention.
- **The personalized paragraph cites a specific moment.** Generic paragraphs go in the folder. Specific ones get reread three times.
- **The named story does load-bearing work in the one-pager.** "Cost of the program: 1/20th of one bad-manager mis-hire" survives a retelling. "ROI was strong" doesn't.
