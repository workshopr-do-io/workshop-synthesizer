# Training Debrief Recipe — Pitfalls

Five ways a training debrief fails.

## 1. The "everyone moved" comfort cluster

**Symptom:** The synthesis lead, wanting to deliver a positive program write-up, frames everyone as having moved. Four of sixteen participants actually moved less than the cohort average. The cohort analysis hides this. The program owner believes the next cohort will perform the same.

**Fix:** The flags section is **mandatory.** If you can't name at least one participant who moved less, you haven't looked. The program owner needs the candor to run the next cohort better.

## 2. Generic personalized paragraphs

**Symptom:** The personalized paragraph for participant five sounds a lot like the one for participant nine. Both read *I saw real growth in your willingness to engage hard conversations.* That sentence has nothing in it.

**Fix:** The personalized paragraph must contain **at least one specific moment** from the program. *"On day two, when you ran the role-play with Maria, the way you paused before responding was the moment I want to point to."* Specific moments make the paragraph survivable. Without them, the document goes in the folder. See [`personalized-paragraph-template.md`](personalized-paragraph-template.md).

## 3. Confusing artifacts produced with outcomes achieved

**Symptom:** The participants produced 30-60-90 plans. The synthesis writes *each participant produced a 30-60-90 plan.* That's an output, not an outcome. The outcome is *will the plans get executed.*

**Fix:** The cohort analysis names whose plans look executable and whose don't, based on specificity, owner clarity, and the participant's stated intent at close. Some plans are clearly going to ship. Some are clearly going to die. The synthesis says which is which.

## 4. The budget one-pager that's all flattery

**Symptom:** Pure success documents lose credibility fast. A budget-holder who reads three glowing one-pagers in a row stops believing the fourth.

**Fix:** Every one-pager names at least one thing that didn't work, even briefly. *The managing-ambiguity sessions landed less strongly than the difficult-conversations sessions. We'd revise those for the next cohort.* That half-sentence makes the whole document more trustworthy.

## 5. Skipping the demonstration-vs-discussion distinction

**Symptom:** Participants *discussed* the difficult-conversations framework. They also *role-played* it. The program owner needs to know which is the load-bearing signal.

- *Discussed* is low-quality — politeness, intellectual engagement, or vapor
- *Demonstrated* is high-quality — they did the thing under pressure with another human watching

**Fix:** Every competency analyzed in the cohort report distinguishes the two. *Twelve of sixteen demonstrated the difficult-conversations framework in role-play with high facility* is meaningful. *Sixteen of sixteen discussed difficult-conversations frameworks* is not.

## Internal-facilitator-specific pitfalls

When the participants are colleagues, the personalized paragraphs and the flags section get politically loaded:

- **Personalized paragraphs:** lead with what the person did well, end with the development direction. Never end on the critical edge.
- **Flags section:** *"came into the program with unclear goals"* lands differently from *"attended reluctantly."* The first invites a conversation; the second invites a personnel decision.
- **One-pager versions:** keep one with named stories for the budget-holder's eyes only; one with all stories anonymized for any wider distribution. **Be explicit about which version goes where.**
