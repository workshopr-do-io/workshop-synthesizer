# The Personalized Paragraph Template

The single most authorship-heavy moment in any recipe in this book. Three to five sentences per participant, citing one specific moment from the program.

The participant will know if it's generic. The participant will keep the paragraph if it isn't.

## The template

```
[Name], the thing I most want to name from our [N] days together is
[specific observation about the participant in a specific moment].
The frame you're working on this quarter — [their current management
challenge] — is going to test [specific competency]. What I'd most
recommend you practice between now and your 30-day check-in is
[one concrete practice]. The one I want to flag for the longer arc
is [one bigger development direction].
```

Three to five sentences. Specific moment. Concrete practice. Honest.

## What makes a good personalized paragraph

### A specific moment, not a general impression

- **Bad:** *I saw real growth in your willingness to engage hard conversations.*
- **Good:** *On day two, when you ran the role-play with Maria, the way you paused before responding was the moment I want to point to.*

The good version cites: a day, a person, a specific behavior. The bad version says nothing.

### Tied to the participant's current work

- **Bad:** *Continue working on giving feedback.*
- **Good:** *The frame you're working on this quarter — restructuring your team after the layoffs — is going to test that capacity to pause.*

The good version names the actual challenge the participant is facing. The participant feels seen.

### One concrete practice, not a list

- **Bad:** *Continue practicing the framework. Schedule weekly 1:1s. Document your feedback. Ask for feedback.*
- **Good:** *What I'd most recommend you practice between now and your 30-day check-in is to keep doing what you did in that pause.*

Four practices means the participant does zero. One concrete practice means the participant might actually try it.

### Optional: the longer arc

If you've read something about the participant's career trajectory or growth direction, you can flag it in one sentence. *The one I want to flag for the longer arc is the bigger question of whether you want to manage at all.* But only if it's based on something specific you saw, not speculation.

## What to avoid

- **Generic praise.** "Real growth," "great energy," "willingness to engage." Strip.
- **Banned vocabulary.** *Leverage, robust, comprehensive, key, critical, strategic, optimize.* The book is engineered against generic prose; the personalized paragraph is no exception.
- **Sentences that could apply to anyone.** If you could swap the name and the paragraph would still work, rewrite.
- **Sentences that flatter without specificity.** *You really came alive in this program.* Means nothing.

## The aloud test for personalized paragraphs

Read each paragraph aloud, imagining yourself as the participant. Does it land? Does it sound specific to that person? Would they read it twice?

If you stumble — generic phrase, broken rhythm, list of platitudes — rewrite. Each paragraph takes 10–20 minutes to write well. Sixteen participants is 4–8 hours of writing. Block the time.

## What the personalized paragraph is NOT

- Not a performance review. Don't grade. Don't rate. Don't compare to other participants.
- Not a recommendation for promotion. That's a different document.
- Not a flag for poor performance. The flags section in the cohort analysis handles that. The personalized paragraph stays constructive even when the participant moved less than the cohort.
- Not the same length for every participant. Some participants will earn a slightly longer paragraph if you saw more in the room. That's fine.

## When the participant is a colleague (internal facilitation)

Two adjustments:

1. **Lead with what they did well.** End with the development direction. Never end on the critical edge.
2. **The paragraph may be read by their manager.** Calibrate accordingly. Specific is still better than generic, but be conscious of who else might see it.
