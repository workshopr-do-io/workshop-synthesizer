# Strategic Offsite Recipe — Pitfalls

Five ways an exec offsite synthesis fails, in book order.

## 1. AI-voiced executive prose

**Symptom:** The CEO memo reads like a McKinsey deck. *Strategic imperatives, robust frameworks, comprehensive review.* Every word is generic. The CEO reads it and feels nothing.

**Fix:** Read the draft aloud in the voice of the CEO. If a sentence doesn't sound like something they'd say to their spouse over dinner, rewrite. Executive prose is plain. Generic prose is fancy.

## 2. Trusting the model to read leadership dynamics

**Symptom:** You ask the model to "characterize the team's alignment on this call." It produces a confident, smooth answer. You believe it because it sounds informed. It is not informed.

**Fix:** Never ask the model to characterize leadership dynamics. Use it for restating decisions, drafting structured content, and arguing the other side. Anything that requires reading the people in the room, you do.

## 3. Manufactured balance

**Symptom:** Your political read says the room committed to direction A. Your synthesis frames it as *"the room explored two directions and is leaning toward A."* That phrasing is a comfort move — you're hedging in case the room shifts later.

**Fix:** If the room committed, write committed. If you're hedging because you're nervous about being wrong, your political read is incomplete. Sharpen it before you ship. The CEO can't act on "leaning toward."

## 4. Confidential framing leakage

**Symptom:** A line that belongs in the confidential appendix shows up in the memo, or worse, in the deck. The CFO sees it. The relationship sours.

**Fix:** The three-artifact altitude pass (Step 7) catches this. Do not skip it. Read all three artifacts back-to-back. The leaks are usually obvious.

## 5. Quoting the room

**Symptom:** You include a direct quote from an executive in the memo or deck. It's accurate — exactly what they said. They read it and feel exposed, because executive conversation isn't supposed to be quotable.

**Fix:** Paraphrase. *The CFO raised cost concerns* rather than *"This costs too much,"* the CFO said. The exception is the CEO, whose own words can appear in any artifact addressed to them. Other executives, never.
