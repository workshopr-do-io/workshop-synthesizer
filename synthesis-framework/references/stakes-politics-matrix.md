# The Stakes × Politics Matrix

A cleaner way to think about when to leave AI on and when to switch it off.

## The matrix

|  | Low politics | High politics |
|---|---|---|
| **Low stakes** | AI on for everything | AI on for [CLUSTER] and [NARRATE]; off for [INTERPRET] and [PRIORITIZE] |
| **High stakes** | AI on for [CLUSTER] and [NARRATE] drafts; you author [INTERPRET] and [PRIORITIZE] | AI on for [CLUSTER] only; everything else by hand |

## The two axes

**Stakes** — *what does it cost if this is wrong?*

- A discovery sprint for a startup pre-product-market-fit is **high stakes**; the company is betting the next six months on it.
- A retro at a fifteen-person team is **lower stakes**; one bad action item won't sink anyone.

**Politics** — *how much does the framing depend on who is in the room?*

- A discovery sprint with a clear sponsor and an aligned exec team is **low politics**.
- A strategic offsite at the C-level is **high politics**.
- A retro on a team that just lost two senior people is **high politics in disguise**.

## The four quadrants, in plain language

### Low stakes / Low politics — *AI on for everything*

You're synthesizing a routine workshop with friendly stakeholders. The cost of a slightly-off finding is low. Use the model freely; spot-check the call slide.

Examples: routine team retro, internal workshop, low-pressure ideation round.

### Low stakes / High politics — *AI on for cluster and narrate; off for interpret and prioritize*

The room dynamics matter, but the consequences are limited. Use the model for the mechanical work; do the political reading by hand.

Examples: team retro after personnel friction, exec sync where one leader is positioning for departure, cross-functional alignment with tension.

### High stakes / Low politics — *AI on for cluster and narrate drafts; you author interpret and prioritize*

The findings carry real weight, but the room agrees on the question. The model helps with volume; you carry the interpretation and the call.

Examples: founder-stage discovery sprint, technical-decision workshop, post-mortem on a contained incident.

### High stakes / High politics — *AI on for cluster only; everything else by hand*

The hardest quadrant. The room is loaded, the stakes are loaded, and the synthesis has to read both correctly. The model can do the mechanical clustering of stickies if there are any. Everything else is human work.

Examples: executive offsite, board strategy day, post-mortem on a politically-charged failure, founder/board working session.

## How to use the matrix

**Before you start any synthesis, put a pin in the matrix.** *This engagement is high stakes, low politics — bottom left.*

Then set the model layer accordingly. **Do not make the matrix decision in the middle of synthesis.** Make it before you start, when you're calm.

The decision should be written down — in the Reference section of your synthesis-ready file.

## Common mistakes

- **Pinning to the wrong quadrant because the workshop "felt easy."** Low-key vibes don't mean low stakes. A retro that ended in laughs can still be a high-politics synthesis if a key person is signaling departure.
- **Re-pinning mid-synthesis because the recipe got hard.** If you're tired and want to outsource [INTERPRET], the matrix is supposed to stop you. Don't move yourself to a lower-stakes quadrant to justify the shortcut.
- **Treating "high stakes / high politics" as the default.** Most workshops aren't this. If you put yourself in the hardest quadrant for every engagement, you'll burn out. Pin honestly.
